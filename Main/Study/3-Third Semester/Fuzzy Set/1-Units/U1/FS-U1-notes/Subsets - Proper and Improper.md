## Main Points
- Proper Subset - all elements of A is in B for A being a Proper subset
$$A\subset B$$
- Improper Subset - Usually means that `A=B` all elements of A is in B and vice versa **BUT** here we can use it to represent a proper subset or `A=B`
---

## 🎯 The Core Reason

The symbol
$$A \subseteq B$$
means:

👉 “A is a subset of B (either proper OR equal)”

So it **includes both cases**:

* Proper subset
* Improper subset

---

## 🤔 Then why do teachers use ⊆ for proper subset?

Because in **most problems**, we only care about:

👉 “All elements of A are inside B”

We usually **don’t care** whether A = B or A ⊂ B.

So teachers use:
$$A \subseteq B$$
as a **general condition** (safe and always correct).

---

## ⚠️ Important Detail (Where confusion comes)

There are **two conventions in math**:

### 🔹 Convention 1 (Most common in exams/books)

* $$\subseteq$$ → subset (can be equal)
* $$\subset$$ → *sometimes* used for proper subset

### 🔹 Convention 2 (Less strict usage)

* $$\subset$$ → used for both subset and proper subset (informal usage)

👉 That’s why your teacher may just use ⊆ everywhere — it's **unambiguous and safe**.

---

## ✅ What YOU should do in exams

✔ If the question says **subset** → use
$$\subseteq$$

✔ If the question clearly says **proper subset** → use
$$\subset$$

---

## 🧠 Simple Rule (Exam-safe)

* Use **⊆ always unless specifically told “proper”**
* Use **⊂ only when you want to emphasize NOT equal**

---

## 💡 Example to make it crystal clear

Let A = {1,2}
Let B = {1,2,3}

* A ⊆ B ✅ (true)
* A ⊂ B ✅ (true)

Let A = {1,2,3}
Let B = {1,2,3}

* A ⊆ B ✅ (true)
* A ⊂ B ❌ (false)
